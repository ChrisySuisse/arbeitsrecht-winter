Gender equality is a principle to which many readily subscribe and which companies are often keen to emphasise. Yet when it comes to concrete decisions – recruitment, promotion, pay, pregnancy or dealing with sexual harassment – the reality can sometimes look quite different. Precisely where equality has practical consequences, what seemed to be a matter of principle can surprisingly quickly become a secondary concern.

Equality in the workplace, however, is not a nice-to-have or merely a matter of good corporate culture. It is a legal right.

The Swiss Gender Equality Act prohibits direct and indirect discrimination based on gender. This applies not only to pay, but throughout the entire employment relationship – from recruitment and working conditions to training, promotion and termination. Discrimination related to pregnancy, marital status or family circumstances may also be legally relevant. Sexual harassment in the workplace is expressly considered a form of discrimination.

Such cases are often complex. Discrimination is rarely stated openly. Comparisons with other employees, the timing and sequence of events, internal communications or the actual application of ostensibly neutral rules may therefore be crucial. For certain forms of discrimination, the Gender Equality Act consequently provides for a reduced burden of proof.

I have dealt with complex gender equality cases for many years and have successfully enforced such claims in court. Where there are indications of discrimination, I do not treat them merely as a secondary aspect of an employment dispute, but assess and pursue them as claims in their own right – both in negotiations and before the courts.

Conversely, not every allegation of discrimination is well founded. I also advise employers faced with such allegations who are committed to addressing them professionally, including by providing legal guidance and support in internal investigations.

Gender equality, protection against discrimination and the protection of sexual integrity are not merely symbolic concerns. They are part of an employer’s concrete legal obligations – and rights that employees are entitled to have respected.
