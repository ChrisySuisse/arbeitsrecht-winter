Fathers’ rights

“Fathers’ rights” are not a separate category of family law. And, in principle, there should be no need for such a category at all. Swiss law does not distinguish between first- and second-class parents. As parents, fathers and mothers have equal rights under the law.

Experience shows, however, that legal equality does not automatically eliminate every inequality in practice. Unexamined or supposedly self-evident assumptions about who is primarily responsible for a child, who determines the child’s everyday life or which parent is initially considered more trustworthy in a conflict can still play a role today. Fathers therefore continue to experience situations in which they bear all the responsibilities of parenthood while their actual role as a parent is less readily recognised.

For precisely this reason, this area of practice has been particularly important to me since I established my law firm.

The issues involved extend far beyond maintenance. They frequently concern joint parental responsibility, custody and care arrangements, each parent’s share of childcare, and personal contact with the child. Cases may involve establishing shared or alternating care arrangements, participation in important decisions, difficulties in maintaining contact or contact being prevented altogether, or situations in which a father is gradually pushed out of his child’s life. In other cases, the circumstances are sufficiently serious that it is necessary to consider whether sole parental responsibility, a change in custody or restrictions on the other parent’s contact are required to protect the child.

Risks to a child are not determined by gender either. What matters is not whether a parent is the mother or the father, but the best interests of the child and the specific circumstances of the case. A father may therefore be the parent who needs to protect the child, and his assessment of the situation must be taken seriously accordingly.

I have represented fathers in such disputes, often over extended periods and through intensive and emotionally demanding proceedings, and I am in regular contact with fathers’ organisations in Switzerland. Time and again, I see fathers having to explain and justify their share of childcare, their involvement in important decisions and sometimes even their fundamental role in their children’s lives to a greater extent than mothers. I take this asymmetry seriously. Where unexamined gender roles or entrenched structures stand in the way of genuinely equal parenthood, I address this clearly and represent my clients’ rights consistently. This is not about family law directed against mothers. It is about family law in which fathers do not first have to prove that they are equal parents, but are treated as what they are: parents.
